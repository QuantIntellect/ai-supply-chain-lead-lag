AI Supply-Chain Lead-Lag

How the companies in NVIDIA's five-layer AI stack move when one of them reports, what their filings and calls say about demand, and which of those patterns still make money once you test them properly.

What holds up

    The result is a map of the chain rather than a trading signal. Use the same-day betas to size and hedge positions around a report. Use the capex and order leads to forecast revenue one to three quarters ahead. Don't expect to profit from buying or selling a stock in the days after a supply-chain partner reports: once you measure against AI stocks instead of the S&P 500 and pay trading costs, that edge is gone.

    The map

    At GTC Berlin on 21 October 2026, Jensen Huang described AI as a five-layer cake: energy powers chips, chips fill AI factories, factories run models, and models power applications. His "Building Europe's AI Economy" slide put European names in each layer. Most of them are private. The ones you can trade are Schneider Electric, Siemens, Siemens Energy, Rolls-Royce, NVIDIA, Deutsche Telekom, Nokia and Nebius.

    A stock's reaction to someone else's earnings depends on the whole chain behind that slide, so this study widens it to the listed companies that build global AI capacity: companies in ten layers across the US, Europe and Asia. The Asian names (TSMC's Taiwan line, SK Hynix, Samsung, Foxconn and the Taiwanese server makers, Tokyo Electron, Advantest, SoftBank) are signal-only: they appear as reporters and as a reference, and are left out of the trading tests, which use US and European stocks.

    Who moves when someone reports

    Each of the earnings releases has an exact time stamp from SEC filing records, the companies' own European regulatory announcements or exchange calendars. For every other stock I take the first trading session on its own exchange that closes after the release. A Microsoft report at 16:05 New York time therefore lands on the next session in Taipei, Frankfurt and New York alike. On that session I measure the stock's market-adjusted return and regress it on the reporter's own market-adjusted move.

They explain only a small part of any single day's move (correlation ), so they work as a hedge ratio, not a forecast.

    The four large cloud platforms plus Oracle spent billion dollars on capital expenditure in , against about 44 billion at the end of 2023 (cash capex from their 10-Q filings, finance leases excluded). That spending reaches suppliers in a fixed order. To see it without the shared upward trend that makes every AI series look correlated, I compare quarter-to-quarter changes in year-on-year growth.

    GPU and custom-chip revenue moves first: NVIDIA, AMD and Broadcom accelerate about a quarter before the cloud companies' capex does. TSMC, Dell and Eaton move in the same quarter. Fabless network silicon, optical modules, power and cooling follow two to three quarters later: Vertiv (+2 quarters), Fabrinet (+2), Monolithic Power (+2), Marvell (+3), Arista (+3), Ciena (+3), Quanta Services (+3).

    Management demand tone by layer

    Two reading passes went through every call digest since 2022 and logged each concrete demand statement as positive (guidance raise, order acceleration, record backlog, supply constraint, capacity expansion) or negative (cut, digestion, push-out, inventory correction, customer loss), with a verbatim quote: statements in all. The share of positive statements per layer and quarter shows the 2022–23 slump moving through the chain and the 2025–26 shortage phase.

    The order of events, 2022–2026

    Read in date order, the call notes show the same cascade the numbers show: shocks start at one end of the chain and reach the other end two to four quarters later.

    1. Jul 2022

      The downturn starts in consumer chips. TSMC, Seagate, Lam Research, Intel and KLA cut their outlooks between 14 and 28 July.

    2. Jul 2022 – Aug 2023

      Cloud digestion. Azure growth slows from 40% to 31%, AWS from 33% to 12%, and Google holds 2023 capex flat. Suppliers follow: Western Digital (Oct 2022), Marvell (Dec 2022), AMD (Jan 2023), Arista and Cisco (May–Jul 2023).

    3. Mar – Oct 2023

      The AI turn. Broadcom puts a number on AI networking (2 Mar), NVIDIA guides to $11bn (24 May), Microsoft says it will accelerate investment (Jul) and TSMC flags a CoWoS shortage (20 Jul). Marvell, Lam, KLA and Coherent follow within about two quarters, AMD on 31 Oct.

    4. Oct 2023 – Sep 2024

      AI spending crowds out the rest. Seagate (Oct 2023) and Intel (Jan 2024) report weak non-AI demand; Broadcom's non-AI business bottoms in Sep 2024.

    5. Jan – Apr 2024

      Memory and storage lead the equipment recovery: Lam (24 Jan), Western Digital (25 Jan), Applied Materials (15 Feb), KLA (25 Apr).

    6. Jul 2024 onward

      Cloud capacity falls short of demand on almost every hyperscaler call. The one easing call (Microsoft, Jan 2025) reverses by Oct 2025.

    7. Oct – Dec 2025

      Shortage. NVIDIA says the clouds are sold out (19 Nov), Broadcom reports a $73bn AI backlog (11 Dec) and Micron's HBM is sold out (17 Dec). Multi-year supply contracts follow at Micron (Mar 2026) and SanDisk (Aug 2026).

    8. Dec 2025 – 2026

      Contracted backlogs jump with frontier-lab deals: Oracle's remaining performance obligations go from $130bn (Mar 2025) to $638bn, Microsoft's reach $678bn (45% OpenAI), Amazon $496bn, Alphabet $462bn, CoreWeave $104bn.

    9. 2026

      The bottleneck moves to power and components. GE Vernova's gas-turbine pipeline grows from 55 to 100 GW with 2029–30 nearly sold (22 Apr). Memory becomes the binding constraint for Dell, HPE and Super Micro. GPU rental renewals price higher (Oracle about +20%, CoreWeave about +25%). Schneider's data-center demand grows at triple-digit rates in Q2 and Q3, and ABB's orders rise 40% in Q3.

    10. Oct 2026

      Mixed signals at the top of the chain. Meta narrows 2026 capex to $135–145bn, stresses efficiency and gives no 2027 number (21 Oct); nVent's orders slowed in July. Equipment makers keep raising: ASML guides 2026 to €44–45bn as customers raise their forecasts (14 Oct), and Lam sees 2027 wafer-fab spending above $200bn (21 Oct).

    What a backtest keeps

    The trading question is whether a stock keeps drifting after a supplier or customer reports, so that you can still trade after the news is out. Every test enters at the first close after the reporter's reaction session ends, which avoids using a European or Asian price before a US move was knowable. Costs are 20 bp per round trip in the US and 30 bp in Europe. Two benchmarks are used: the local index, and an equal-weight basket of the AI stocks in the same region. The second matters. These stocks rose together from 2023 to 2026, so any strategy that is mostly long them looks good against the S&P 500.

    The combined model

    A walk-forward regression combines all of it: customer, supplier, same-layer and other companies' recent release reactions, each stock's own last reaction and its recent return.     Method and caveats

    • Prices. Daily prices from 2 June 2014 to 22 October 2026 for every stock and benchmark. Abnormal return is the return minus a 250-day rolling beta (lagged 21 days) times the local index: S&P 500 ETF, STOXX 600, TAIEX, KOSPI or Nikkei. The second measure uses an equal-weight basket of the region's tradeable AI stocks instead. History before a listing is dropped where the price series belonged to a SPAC, tracking stock or unrelated predecessor (Vertiv, Oklo, NuScale, Cipher, TeraWulf, Dell).
    • Release times. US releases use the SEC "Accepted" stamp from each 8-K filing index page. The JSON field acceptanceDateTime is offset by 4 to 10 hours, so it was not used. European releases use the companies' own regulatory announcement stamps, collected per company. Yahoo dates fill the remaining gaps and are checked against abnormal volume. Releases from EDGAR traded at a median 2.8 times normal volume on the reaction day.
    • Contamination. A responder is dropped from a reporter's event if it reported within one day itself. Drift tests also drop it if it reported within the next 30 days.
    • Hypotheses. The primary hypotheses were the stability of the sympathy map, drift from customer to supplier and from supplier to customer, the earnings cascade, and TSMC's monthly sales. Hold-out years were fixed in advance: fit through 2024, test 2025–26. I wrote the hypotheses down after the first pooled regressions, so they are not formally pre-registered. Roughly 60 regression specifications were run in total, so expect three or four of them to clear |t| > 2 by chance. The conclusions above rest on patterns that hold in both periods or under both benchmarks.
    • Survivorship. The universe was chosen in October 2026 and is made of the AI build-out's winners. That biases any "long AI stocks" result upward, which is why the AI-basket benchmark and the dollar-neutral versions matter. Relative tests are much less affected.
    • Coverage gaps. Motley Fool transcripts cover calls but miss some quarters, for example NVIDIA in February 2024 and May–August 2025, Meta in 2023, and Micron in 2021–23. The site rate-limited further downloads. European companies' own transcripts and documents fill most of their gaps. EPS surprises come from Yahoo. Supply links marked "industry knowledge" are unverified.
    • YouTube. The keynote video itself could not be fetched (YouTube's bot check), so the keynote record comes from NVIDIA's live blogs and press kits: keynotes and documents.
